Six cruxes

Mathematical Thinking

You spent years learning to recognise which procedure a problem wants. That skill runs out. What replaces it is the ability to read a statement for exactly what it says, and to know what would settle whether it is true.

This is that ability, taken apart. Six rungs, each standing on the one below it. By the end you should be able to take a claim in English, write down its formal skeleton, and know from the skeleton alone what a proof of it has to do — which is precisely what Lean will ask of you.

  1. Why precision isn’t fussinessstart here

    Five lines of algebra you can check yourself, ending at 1 = −1. Every link looks fine. Find the broken one.

    Stands on everyday language and school algebra  ·  pays off as the four forms and three combinators that every mathematical statement is built from

  2. The implication table is forced, not chosen

    Build the truth table for “if φ then ψ” yourself, and watch every alternative to the standard one die.

    Stands on rung 1  ·  pays off as the table that will later license proof by contradiction

  3. Quantifier order is meaning

    A fact sheet that accidentally condemns one man to die every hour, forever.

    Stands on rung 2  ·  pays off as readable epsilon–delta, and negation as a crank you turn

  4. The form of a statement is the proof’s blueprint

    Two statements you cannot yet prove. Read only their shapes, and write the first three lines of each proof anyway.

    Stands on rungs 2 and 3  ·  pays off as the Lean-shaped skill

  5. The indirect moves, and why they’re legal

    Proof by contradiction feels like cheating. Derive your licence to use it from a table you already built.

    Stands on rungs 2 and 4  ·  pays off as √2 irrational, and induction as a move you could have invented

  6. The hole in the rationals

    The fractions look like they fill the number line completely. Prove that they don’t.

    Stands on everything  ·  pays off as the reason anyone built any of this

Two things deliberately left out: sets, relations and functions, and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. Both are real mathematics. Neither is load-bearing for the goal above — nothing on this ladder needs them — and putting them in would buy coverage at the price of depth. They sit downstream of rung 5; you will be able to build them yourself.

Go slowly. The measure of progress here is not lessons finished but ideas you can regenerate cold, a week later, without the page in front of you.
