Why the chalk knows
which plane to break on

Get a stick of blackboard chalk. I meant it last time and I mean it now — this lesson starts with your hands, not your eyes.

Hold it at both ends and pull until it snaps.

Look at the break. It's flat. Square across, ninety degrees to the axis, a clean disc. Do it again with a fresh piece and you'll get the same flat face. Everybody does.

Now take another piece. Don't pull it — twist it. Grip each end and turn your hands in opposite directions until it goes.

That break is not flat. It's a spiral, running around the stick at roughly forty-five degrees, and if you look at the two pieces they screw back together like a bottle cap. Twist the other way and you get a spiral leaning the other way — the mirror image, the other diagonal.

So here is the question this whole lesson answers.

The chalk is not smart.
What is it responding to?

Whatever quantity decides where a material tears, that quantity is clearly different on different planes at the same point in the same stick. So it cannot be a single number attached to the point. Let me go and find what it actually is.

First, honour the thing we're about to break

Where \(P/A\) came from, and why it was a triumph

Before I take stress apart, I want you to see why "force divided by area" was a genuinely great idea, because the temptation is to think it was naive. It wasn't.

Suppose you want to characterise a material — steel, say — so that anyone can predict how a steel part will stretch. The obvious experiment: take a steel rod, pull it with force \(F\), measure how much it lengthens, \(\delta\). Plot \(F\) against \(\delta\). You get a straight line (for small stretches). Its slope is the stiffness.

Now take a thicker steel rod and repeat. You get a different line — steeper, because it's harder to stretch. A thinner rod: shallower line. A longer rod: shallower again.

See the problem before I say it

You want to hand an engineer a single sheet of paper that tells them everything about steel. With \(F\)-versus-\(\delta\) graphs, what do you have to hand them?

Why this is hopeless

A different graph for every cross-section and every length. Which is not a property of steel — it's a property of that particular rod. To cover all possible parts you'd need infinitely many experiments, and you'd have characterised nothing.

The fix took a lifetime to find. Jacob Bernoulli got there in his final paper, published in 1705, the year he died. Same experiments, same rods, same measurements — but plot them differently. On the vertical axis put \(F/A\), force divided by the cross-sectional area. On the horizontal axis put \(\delta/L\), the stretch divided by the original length.

The three lines collapse onto one.

Thick rod, thin rod, long rod — one line. That line is a property of steel, not of any rod. One experiment characterises the material, forever, for every part anyone will ever make from it. The vertical quantity got the name stress, the horizontal one strain, and essentially all of engineering mechanics is downstream of that regraphing.

Why I'm telling you the date

Not for trivia. Because \(F/A\) looks so obvious in hindsight that it's easy to assume it must have been obvious then — and if you believe the easy things were easy, you'll assume your own confusion means you're slow.

A first-rate mathematician spent a career getting to "divide by the area," and it was the last thing he published. When you get stuck in this course, that's the company you're in.

So \(F/A\) earned its place. But look at what it quietly smuggled in.

The crack in the floor

\(A\) is the area of which surface?

When you write \(\sigma = F/A\) for a rod under tension, \(A\) is the cross-sectional area — the area of a slice taken square across the rod. Nobody ever says so. It's assumed, because it's the obvious place to cut.

But you already know from rung 0 that the cut is free. You can slice at any angle you like, and equilibrium still holds. So let me slice the rod at an angle \(\theta\) instead, and ask the same question.

Two things change at once, and we walked through both last time:

  • The slanted face is larger than the square one. Bigger \(A\), so a smaller force per unit area.
  • The force is no longer perpendicular to the face. Part of it presses into the face; part of it drags along the face. Those are physically different things — one tries to pull the material apart, the other tries to make it slide.

So "the stress at this point" gave a different number, of a different character, purely because I moved my pencil. And now the chalk makes sense as a question: if the material fails when some quantity gets too big on some plane, and that quantity varies plane by plane, then the plane the material chooses to break on is the plane where that quantity peaks.

Which means I need the number on every plane, not one.

Building the right object

The stress vector: pick a point, pick a plane, then ask

Let me define the thing carefully, because the definition is where the whole subject turns.

Take a point \(P\) inside a loaded body. Pass an imaginary plane through it — rung 0 says I may. Identify the plane by its outward normal \(\mathbf n\), the little arrow sticking out of it; that's how you name a plane with a direction.

Now, instead of taking the whole cut face, take only a tiny patch of area \(\Delta A\) hugging the point \(P\). Across that patch the material on the far side pulls on the material on this side with some small resultant force \(\Delta \mathbf T\). Divide, and shrink the patch:

$$\mathbf T^{(n)} \;=\; \lim_{\Delta A \to 0}\frac{\Delta \mathbf T}{\Delta A}$$

That limit is the stress vector on plane \(\mathbf n\) at point \(P\). Force per unit area, but now pinned to one point and one plane, with nothing assumed.

Two things about it deserve a beat each.

It is a vector, and it does not generally point along \(\mathbf n\). This is the thing that trips people. There is no rule saying the material must push straight out of the face you happened to draw. The stress vector points wherever the physics points; your plane is just the question you asked. So resolve it into two parts: the piece along the normal, called \(\sigma\) — the normal stress, pulling apart or squeezing together — and the piece lying in the plane, called \(\tau\) — the shear stress, trying to slide one face across the other.

The shrinking is what buys us "at a point." Rung 0 gave us six numbers for a whole cross-section and I flagged that as a debt: six numbers say nothing about where on the face. Shrinking the patch to a point is how the debt gets paid. It's also why we insist the material is a continuum — if you shrink far enough to hit atoms and gaps, the limit stops meaning anything. That idealization is doing real work here; it isn't decoration.

And now the disaster

How many planes pass through a point?

I've defined the right quantity. Now let me count how much of it I need.

To know "the state of stress at \(P\)" — to be able to predict which plane the chalk chooses — I need \(\mathbf T^{(n)}\) for every plane through \(P\). Not the convenient ones. Every one, because I don't know in advance where the material will give.

So: how many planes pass through a single point?

Tilt a plane by one degree and it's a different plane, with a different stress vector. By a tenth of a degree. By a thousandth. Spin it around the normal — different again. There are as many planes through a point as there are directions in space.

Ten thousand of them. A hundred thousand. There is no largest number here; it is a continuous infinity of planes, each with its own vector, each needing three numbers.

Will your notebook be sufficient?

Sit in that for a second, because it genuinely looks fatal. We started with one clean number, \(F/A\). We showed it was wrong. And the honest replacement appears to be an infinite table that no one could ever write down, measure, or store — for a single point, of which a real object contains infinitely many.

If the answer here were "yes, it's infinite, sorry," there would be no such subject as strength of materials. Engineering would have stopped in 1705.

Your turn — and this is the whole lesson

Suppose I hand you the stress vector on a small number of planes through \(P\), chosen by me, and ask you to work out the stress vector on some other plane you've never seen — one at a peculiar angle, of your choosing.

How many planes would I have to give you? Guess a number and commit to it. Then ask yourself the harder question: what tool would you use to get from the ones you were given to the one you want?

You already own that tool. You used it in rung 0.

The rescue

Three. It's three. And a triangle proves it.

The answer is three — any three mutually perpendicular planes. Know the stress vector on those, and you can compute it on any of the infinitely many others, exactly, with arithmetic. Augustin-Louis Cauchy published this in 1822, and it is the reason this subject exists.

And the tool that gets you there is the one from rung 0: cut out a piece and balance it. Watch.

I'll do it in two dimensions, where you can see everything. Three dimensions is the same argument with one more face and no new ideas.

Take the point \(P\). Cut a tiny wedge out around it — a right triangle. Two of its faces are the coordinate planes (one with normal along \(-x\), one along \(-y\)); the third, the hypotenuse, is the arbitrary plane I actually care about, with outward normal \(\mathbf n\) tilted at angle \(\theta\) from the \(x\)-axis.

Let the hypotenuse have length \(L\) (take unit thickness, so length is area). Then simple trigonometry on a right triangle gives the other two:

  • the vertical face (normal along \(-x\)) has length \(L\cos\theta\);
  • the horizontal face (normal along \(-y\)) has length \(L\sin\theta\).

Check it at \(\theta = 0\): the normal \(\mathbf n\) points along \(x\), so the hypotenuse is the vertical face — and sure enough \(L\cos 0 = L\), while the horizontal face has length zero. The wedge has collapsed to a line, correctly.

What those cosines really are

\(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) here are the components of the unit normal: \(\mathbf n = (n_x, n_y) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). So the rule reads: the face perpendicular to \(x\) has area \(L\,n_x\).

That's a shadow. Hold a card up to a light and the shadow it casts is its own area times the cosine of how far it's tilted. Each coordinate face is the slanted face's shadow cast along that axis.

Stated that way it carries over to three dimensions unchanged — the \(x\)-face of the tetrahedron has area \(A\,n_x\) — which is why the 3D proof needs no new idea, only more writing.

Now balance the wedge. Force on a face is stress × area. Take the \(x\)-direction, and remember the stresses on the two coordinate faces point in the negative directions because those are negative faces:

$$T^{(n)}_x \, L \;-\; \sigma_{xx}\,(L\cos\theta) \;-\; \tau_{yx}\,(L\sin\theta) \;=\; 0$$

Divide through by \(L\). Every \(L\) cancels — the size of the wedge never mattered:

$$T^{(n)}_x = \sigma_{xx}\cos\theta + \tau_{yx}\sin\theta$$

Do the same in \(y\) and you get \(T^{(n)}_y = \tau_{xy}\cos\theta + \sigma_{yy}\sin\theta\).

That's it. That's Cauchy's formula. Two lines of bookkeeping on a triangle, and the infinite table is gone.

Where did gravity go?

The wedge has weight, and I dropped it. That wasn't laziness — watch the scaling.

The face forces are stress × area, so they scale like \(L\). The weight is density × volume, so it scales like \(L^2\). Divide everything by \(L\), as we just did, and the face terms stay put while the weight term still carries a factor of \(L\) — and \(L\) is on its way to zero.

So gravity, and any other body force, contributes exactly nothing to the stress state at a point. It matters for what the stresses are, through the equilibrium of the whole body; it plays no part in how they relate plane to plane. Small volumes have negligible weight compared to the surfaces bounding them, and at a point that becomes exact.

Tidying up, and getting a symmetry for free

Four numbers, one object

Look at what Cauchy's formula consumes: \(\sigma_{xx}, \tau_{xy}, \tau_{yx}, \sigma_{yy}\). Four numbers in 2D (nine in 3D). Feed it any direction and it hands back the stress vector on that plane.

The naming is worth ten seconds because it makes the rest of the course readable. Every component carries two subscripts:

  • first subscript = the plane it acts on (named by its normal);
  • second subscript = the direction it points.

So \(\tau_{xy}\) acts on the \(x\)-plane, pointing in \(y\). And \(\sigma\) versus \(\tau\) just tells you at a glance whether the two subscripts match — same means normal stress, different means shear.

Now — those two shear components, \(\tau_{xy}\) and \(\tau_{yx}\). They are not obviously related. One acts on the vertical face pointing up; the other on the horizontal face pointing sideways. Let me show you that they're forced to be equal, using nothing you don't have.

Try it first

Draw a tiny square element around \(P\), sides \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\). Put \(\tau_{xy}\) on both vertical faces and \(\tau_{yx}\) on both horizontal faces, obeying the sign rule (positive direction on the positive face, negative on the negative).

The square isn't spinning. Take moments about its centre.

Equality of cross shears

Look at the two vertical faces. On the right face \(\tau_{xy}\) pushes up; on the left face it pushes down. Equal, opposite, offset — that's a couple, trying to rotate the element anticlockwise. Its size is force × separation: \((\tau_{xy}\,\Delta y)\times \Delta x\).

Now the two horizontal faces. On the top \(\tau_{yx}\) pushes right; on the bottom it pushes left. Another couple — turning the element clockwise, of size \((\tau_{yx}\,\Delta x)\times \Delta y\).

The normal stresses contribute nothing: they act straight through the centre, so they have no lever arm.

The element is not spinning up to infinite speed. So the two couples must cancel:

$$\tau_{xy}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y \;=\; \tau_{yx}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\tau_{xy} = \tau_{yx}}$$

This is called equality of cross shears. You cannot have shear on one face without the same shear appearing on the perpendicular face. Shear always comes in fours.

So there are only three independent numbers in 2D, not four.

And that immediately tidies Cauchy's formula. Notice the \(x\)-equation came out with \(\tau_{yx}\) and the \(y\)-equation with \(\tau_{xy}\) — genuinely different components, sitting in the two equations asymmetrically. Now that we've proved they're equal we may write both as \(\tau_{xy}\), and the moment we do, the two equations line up into a symmetric square array:

$$\begin{bmatrix} T^{(n)}_x \\[2pt] T^{(n)}_y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{xx} & \tau_{xy} \\[2pt] \tau_{xy} & \sigma_{yy}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta \\[2pt] \sin\theta\end{bmatrix}$$

Read it as a sentence: the array eats a direction and returns the stress vector on the plane facing that direction. That array is the stress tensor. It is the honest replacement for \(F/A\), and it is the answer to the infinite table — not because the infinity went away, but because three numbers generate all of it.

The misconception that will bite you

The components are just numbers. The tensor is the thing.

People say "stress is a tensor, not a scalar" and then get confused, because every entry in that array plainly is a plain number — \(\sigma_{xx} = 40\) MPa, no arrows attached. Both are true, and holding them together is the whole idea.

Each component is a scalar. But it is a scalar answer to a question you had to specify, and the subscripts are where the question hides. Strip them off and the number means nothing.

So this sentence is incomplete: "the stress at this point is 40 MPa." Forty on which plane, in which direction? Without that, you haven't said anything — and if you plug it into a failure calculation you may be reading off a plane the material doesn't care about while the plane it does care about is at 45°, carrying something else entirely.

The same \(F/A\), a different slot

A punch press drives a cylindrical punch through sheet metal and knocks out a disc. Force \(F\); the area that resists is the rim of the disc — its circumference times the sheet thickness. Engineers write \(F/A\) and call it the shear stress.

Same three symbols as the tension bar. Completely different physics: there the material was being pulled apart normal to the plane, here it is being sliced along the plane. In the tension bar that number sits on the diagonal of the array; in the punch it sits off the diagonal.

The number doesn't tell you which. The slot does. That is what "stress is a tensor" actually cashes out to.

Now collect

Run the machine on the chalk

Time to use it. Chalk in tension: I pull along \(x\) with force \(F\) over cross-section \(A\). On the square cross-section there's normal stress \(F/A\) and no shear. So the tensor is as simple as it gets — one non-zero entry:

$$\boldsymbol\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} F/A & 0 \\[2pt] 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

It looks like nothing is happening. Three of the four slots are zero. Let's see.

Your turn — do the arithmetic, it's four lines

Ask about a plane tilted at angle \(\theta\), so \(\mathbf n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). Note \(\theta = 0\) is the ordinary square cross-section.

(a) Multiply the array by \(\mathbf n\) to get \(\mathbf T^{(n)}\).
(b) The normal stress is the part of \(\mathbf T\) along \(\mathbf n\) — take the dot product \(\sigma_n = \mathbf T\cdot\mathbf n\).
(c) The shear is what's left over: \(\tau_n = \sqrt{|\mathbf T|^2 - \sigma_n^2}\).

Then the payoff question: at which \(\theta\) is each one biggest?

Work it through

(a) The array has only one live entry, so the multiplication is easy:

$$\mathbf T^{(n)} = \left(\tfrac{F}{A}\cos\theta,\; 0\right)$$

Note it points along \(x\) whatever plane you ask about — of course it does; the material is only being pulled one way. But it is not along \(\mathbf n\) unless \(\theta=0\).

(b) Dot with \(\mathbf n = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\):

$$\sigma_n = \tfrac{F}{A}\cos^2\theta$$

(c) \(\mathbf T\) has only an \(x\)-component and nothing else, so its length simply is that component: \(|\mathbf T| = \frac{F}{A}\cos\theta\). Then

$$\tau_n = \tfrac{F}{A}\cos\theta\sqrt{1-\cos^2\theta} = \tfrac{F}{A}\sin\theta\cos\theta = \tfrac{F}{2A}\sin 2\theta$$

The maxima. \(\sigma_n\) is biggest at \(\theta = 0\), where it equals \(F/A\) — the plane square across the bar.

\(\tau_n\) is biggest where \(\sin 2\theta = 1\), that is \(\theta = 45^\circ\), where it equals \(\dfrac{F}{2A}\) — half the maximum normal stress.

Now read what just happened, because two separate facts fell out of one calculation.

First: the chalk. Chalk is brittle, and brittle things come apart when pulled — they fail on maximum normal stress. Where is normal stress maximum? At \(\theta = 0\): the plane square across the stick. That is exactly where your chalk broke, flat, ninety degrees, every time.

Not a fact I told you. A prediction from a triangle.

Second, and stranger: you applied no shear at all, and there is shear inside. You pulled straight along the axis. Nothing in your hands twisted or sheared anything. Yet there are planes inside that chalk — the ones at 45° — carrying shear stress of \(F/2A\), and it is the largest shear anywhere in the object.

The shear was never applied. It is what "being pulled" looks like when you ask about a tilted plane. Pull is not a property of the material; it's a property of pull-plus-a-plane, and on a tilted plane the same pull reads partly as slide.

A prediction you can check on a metal bar

If shear at 45° is real, then a material that fails by sliding rather than by pulling apart should break at 45° in a plain tension test — even though nobody sheared it.

Ductile metals do exactly this. Pull a mild-steel specimen to destruction and it necks down and fails with the classic cup-and-cone fracture — and the giveaway is the rim: a ring of shear lips running at about 45° to the axis, which is what forms the cone. Same test, same loading as the chalk; a different failure plane, because the two materials are listening to different components of the same tensor.

(The flat patch in the middle of a cup-and-cone gets there by a different route, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise — that's void growth inside the neck, and it belongs to rung 7. The 45° rim is the part this lesson predicts, and it's the part that's there.)

Two materials, one stress state, two different answers to "which plane." That is the clearest possible evidence that the tensor — not any single number — is the real object.

A convention warning, so other books don't confuse you

I measured \(\theta\) to the plane's normal, which is why you got \(\cos^2\theta\). Many books — and Prof. Ramesh in his lectures — measure \(\theta\) to the plane itself, and write \(\sigma_n = \frac{F}{A}\sin^2\theta\).

Identical physics, ninety degrees apart in bookkeeping. The maximum shear is at 45° either way. When you meet \(\sin^2\), check which angle is being measured before you panic.

One honest caveat about "uniform"

I claimed the tension bar has stress \(F/A\) spread evenly over the cross-section. Near the grips that's false — clamp a bar in a machine and the stress right under the jaws is a mess no formula here can touch.

What rescues us is Saint-Venant's principle: if you apply a statically equivalent load — same resultant force, same resultant couple, however it's distributed — then a short distance away the stresses settle into the same pattern regardless. A rule of thumb for "short" is about one cross-sectional width.

So every result in this course is a claim about the region away from where the load is applied. That isn't a weakness; it's the licence that lets us say "uniform" at all. Keep it in your pocket — it comes back in rung 4.

What I still owe you

I can interrogate any plane. One at a time.

Look honestly at what we have. Cauchy's formula answers a question of the form "what is happening on this particular plane?" Ask it, turn the crank, get an answer.

But the question the chalk actually poses is the other one: which plane is the worst? Where does normal stress peak, and how big does it get? Where does shear peak? Are those the same plane or different ones?

For the tension bar I got away with it, because \(\cos^2\theta\) is simple enough to maximise by inspection. That was luck. Give me a general state — some \(\sigma_{xx}\), some \(\sigma_{yy}\), some \(\tau_{xy}\), all non-zero — and finding the worst plane means grinding through trigonometry, or checking planes one at a time forever.

And the chalk is still half unexplained. The twisted stick broke at 45° on a helix. Torsion produces a state of pure shear — no normal stress on the obvious planes at all — and yet a brittle material, which fails on normal stress, found a plane to fail on. Which plane, and why 45°, and why does it flip when you twist the other way?

Both questions have the same answer, and it is a shape.

Plot \(\sigma_n\) horizontally and \(\tau_n\) vertically, and let \(\theta\) sweep through every plane at the point. The dot doesn't wander. It traces a circle — perfectly, exactly, for every stress state there has ever been.

Once you have that circle you don't hunt for the worst plane; you look at it. The extremes are the leftmost and rightmost points, the maximum shear is the top, and the whole infinite family of planes is a single shape you can draw on the back of an envelope. Otto Mohr worked this out for engineers who had no computers and no matrix algebra, and it is still the fastest way to see a stress state whole.

That's rung 2. Bring the two halves of your broken chalk.

Before you feel like you've got this. This lesson had one genuinely hard idea in it — everything else was arithmetic — and the hard idea is that stress belongs to a point and a plane together. It's hard because it asks you to give up a number you'd been treating as real.
