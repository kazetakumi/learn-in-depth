Nine rungs

Strength of Materials

Rigid bodies were a lie you were allowed to tell. Everything bends. This is where you stop pretending.

What you'll be able to do at the end: take a real shaft carrying torsion, bending and axial pull at the same time, find the single worst point in it, write down the complete state of stress there, and say whether it lives or dies — and by what margin.

Every rung stands on the payoff of the rung before it. Nothing floats. If you ever have to trust me for a step, that step is a bug — tell me and I'll go break it down further.
  1. 00✓

    The Cut

    You cannot see inside a solid body. So pretend to slice it — and equilibrium of the leftover piece tells you exactly what the inside is doing.

    Stands on: holding a rope that isn't moving.

  2. 01▶

    Why the chalk knows which plane to break onyou are here

    Chalk pulled apart snaps flat. The same chalk twisted snaps on a 45° helix. The chalk is not smart — so "force ÷ area" cannot be the whole story.

    Stands on: rung 0.

  3. 02·

    The circle

    Rotate the plane you're asking about, and the pair (σ, τ) traces a perfect circle. Why a circle?

    Stands on: rung 1.

  4. 03·

    The bridge: strain, and one tension test

    Statics alone cannot tell you the stress. Two equations, three unknowns. You have to go and ask the material.

    Stands on: rung 2.

  5. 04·

    The recipe — guess the deformation, and the stress falls out

    The real secret of this subject: it dodges differential equations by conjecturing how the body deforms. One machine generates torsion and bending both.

    Stands on: rungs 0–3. This is the keystone.

  6. 05·

    The subtle one: shear in beams

    Stack four loose planks and bend them: the ends fan out like a deck of cards. Glue them and they don't. What is the glue doing?

    Stands on: rung 4 — and it is the one thing the recipe cannot give you.

  7. 06·

    Deflection

    The flexure formula already contains the shape of the bent beam, hidden inside a term nobody looks at.

    Stands on: rung 4.

  8. 07·

    When does it break?

    A tension test hands you one number. A real part has three principal stresses. Something must collapse three into one.

    Stands on: rungs 2 and 3.

  9. 08·

    Buckling — where the undeformed shape lies to you

    Every result above was computed on the shape the body had before loading. For a column in compression, that assumption predicts it never fails.

    Stands on: rung 6.
