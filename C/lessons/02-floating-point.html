C, from zero to architect · Rung 2

Floating point is not the number line

I left a thread hanging last time, and I want to pull it before anything else.

I opened rung 1 with four bytes — db 0f 49 40 — and told you they read as 3.141593 through a float lens. Then in the exercises you wrote float f = 3.14159; yourself and dumped it, and got something almost but not quite the same:

my four bytes:      db 0f 49 40
your 3.14159:       d0 0f 49 40

Two patterns, one byte apart. Ask for more digits and they separate:

db 0f 49 40   ->  3.141592741
d0 0f 49 40   ->  3.141590118

Now look hard at the second one. You typed 3.14159. The machine is holding 3.141590118. Nobody rounded your display; that is the number now sitting in those four bytes. The value you asked for is simply not in there.

And here is the same fact with the polite disguise taken off. Type these two lines and run them:

printf("%.20f\n", 0.1 + 0.2);
printf("%.20f\n", 0.3);
0.30000000000000004441
0.29999999999999998890

So 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 is false. On your machine, on mine, in C, in Python, in JavaScript, in every language you will ever use.

People call this a quirk, a gotcha, a rounding error, a thing to shrug at. It is none of those. It is a consequence of a design decision that you are about to make yourself — because I'm not going to tell you how floating point works. I'm going to make you invent it, and then you'll find you can't have invented it any other way.

Your turn to be the designerThirty-two bits, and fractions are your problem

Rung 1 gave you everything you need for this. A type is a lens: some number of bytes, plus an agreement about how to read them. Integers you can already read — each bit is worth twice the one to its right, and you stop at the ones place.

Now the specification lands on your desk. Thirty-two bits. They must represent fractional numbers. You choose the agreement.

Before reading on — genuinely try this

You already know how to read 32 bits as a whole number. The bits to the left of the ones place are worth 1, 2, 4, 8, 16…

What is the most obvious thing to do to get fractions? You don't need a new idea. You need to keep going in a direction you're already going.

The obvious answer — and it's a real design, still used today

Keep going rightward. If each step left doubles, each step right halves. Put an imaginary point in the middle of your 32 bits: sixteen bits above it worth 1, 2, 4, 8…, sixteen bits below it worth ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16…

  bit 31 ............ bit 16 . bit 15 ............ bit 0
  16384  ...  4  2  1     .    1/2  1/4  ...  1/32768  1/65536

This is called fixed point, and it is a perfectly good design. It's not a toy — it ran the graphics in every game console before hardware floats were cheap, and it still runs audio processing and embedded control loops today. Adding two of them is just integer addition; the point is imaginary, so the hardware doesn't even need to know it's there.

Good. You have a working number format. Let's find out where it dies.

Push on it

Your 16.16 fixed-point format can count up to 32767.99998 and its smallest step is 0.0000152588.

Now go and use it for real work. Try to store the charge of an electron, 0.00000000000000000016. Try to store the distance to the sun in metres, 1.5e11.

What exactly goes wrong — and is it one problem or two?

It's one problem wearing two coats

The electron's charge lands on zero. Your smallest step is about one sixty-five-thousandth, and the number you want is a hundred million million times smaller than that. It isn't approximated badly — it isn't there at all.

The distance to the sun doesn't fit either: your ceiling is thirty-two thousand and change.

But notice they're the same complaint. You nailed the point down in one place, and one place is never right. When your numbers are around 30000, you're spending sixteen bits on resolution finer than a thousandth — resolution nobody asked for and that the top of your range can't use anyway. When your numbers are around a billionth, all sixteen of those bits are still in the wrong neighbourhood.

Precision has to go somewhere, and you've stapled it to a fixed absolute position on the number line rather than to the numbers you actually have.

The fixStop nailing the point down

Say the complaint out loud and the fix says itself: the point is in the wrong place — so store where the point goes.

You have done this since school, in the other base. Nobody writes Avogadro's number as 602214076000000000000000. You write:

6.02214076 × 1023

Two pieces. The digits that carry the accuracy, and an exponent that says how far to slide the point. Change the exponent and the same nine digits describe the mass of a galaxy or the mass of an electron. The precision travels with the number instead of sitting at a fixed address.

Do it in binary and you've invented floating point — the point floats, it isn't fixed:

value = ± 1.fraction × 2exponent

Which tells you exactly what has to be in those 32 bits. One bit for the sign. Some bits for the exponent. The rest for the fraction. Nothing else is needed, and nothing there can be dropped.

Spend the budget

Thirty-two bits. One goes to the sign. You have 31 left to divide between exponent and fraction.

Before you pick numbers: what does each side buy you, and what does taking a bit from one to give to the other cost? Answer that first — then pick a split.

What you're trading, and where the real design landed

Exponent bits buy range — how far from 1 you can get in either direction. Each extra exponent bit squares your reach, because the exponent itself is a power of two.

Fraction bits buy precision — how many significant digits you carry. Each extra fraction bit halves your error.

They come out of the same 31 bits, so every bit of reach costs you a bit of accuracy. There is no clever escape from this; it's a conservation law.

The split the world settled on in 1985, in the IEEE 754 standard, is 8 and 23:

float (32 bits):    1 sign   8 exponent   23 fraction
double (64 bits):   1 sign  11 exponent   52 fraction

Eight exponent bits reach roughly 2±127, which is about 10±38 — and sure enough, your machine reports FLT_MAX = 3.40282e+38. Twenty-three fraction bits give roughly seven significant decimal digits, which is exactly the "about seven digits" that older books quote at you as a fact to memorise. It isn't a fact. It's 23 bits, and log10(224) ≈ 7.2.

Two tricks you should try to catchThe designers got greedy

Let's look at real bits. Here's a small program that prints a float's 32 bits split into the three fields — build it, you'll want it for the rest of this lesson:

#include <stdio.h>
#include <string.h>
#include <stdint.h>

void bits(const char *label, float f) {
    uint32_t u;
    memcpy(&u, &f, 4);                 /* the same trick as rung 1 */
    printf("%-8s", label);
    for (int i = 31; i >= 0; i--) {
        putchar((u >> i) & 1 ? '1' : '0');
        if (i == 31 || i == 23) putchar(' ');   /* split the fields */
    }
    printf("\n");
}
bits("1.0f",  1.0f);
bits("2.0f",  2.0f);
bits("3.0f",  3.0f);
bits("0.5f",  0.5f);
bits("-1.0f", -1.0f);
1.0f    0 01111111 00000000000000000000000
2.0f    0 10000000 00000000000000000000000
3.0f    0 10000000 10000000000000000000000
0.5f    0 01111110 00000000000000000000000
-1.0f   1 01111111 00000000000000000000000
Two things here are not what you'd have built

First: 1.0 has a fraction field of all zeros. So where did the 1 in "1.0" go? It isn't stored anywhere.

Second: 1.0 is 1.0 × 20, so its exponent is zero — but the exponent field reads 01111111, which is 127. And 0.5 is 1.0 × 2−1, whose field reads 126.

Work out both before you open this. The first one has a reason you can derive; the second is a choice, and asking why would anyone do that is the right instinct.

Trick one: the bit you get for free

Write any nonzero number as 1.something × 2k — you always can, because you just slide the point until exactly one digit sits to the left of it. And in binary, the only nonzero digit is 1. So that leading digit is always 1, in every number, forever.

A bit that is always the same value carries no information. So don't store it — agree that it's there and spend the bit on something useful. This is called a normalised representation, and it's why 23 stored fraction bits give you 24 bits of actual precision. One free bit, extracted from a tautology.

Check it against 3.0 above: fraction 1000… means the value is 1.1 in binary — that's 1 + ½ = 1.5 — times 21 = 3. ✓

Trick two: why the exponent is offset by 127

The exponent has to go negative — that's the entire point of the format. The obvious way is to make the exponent field a signed number. Instead they store exponent + 127, so an 8-bit field running 0…255 covers exponents −127…128. Field 127 means 20. This offset is called the bias.

What it buys: with the bias, a bigger exponent is always a bigger bit pattern, read as a plain unsigned integer. So two positive floats can be compared by comparing their raw 32 bits as integers, and you get the right answer — no floating-point hardware needed. Sorting hardware, comparison instructions, and the total ordering of the format all get simpler. Check it in the numbers straight off your machine:

0.1f  ->  1036831949        (rising left to right)
0.5f  ->  1056964608
1.0f  ->  1065353216
1.5f  ->  1069547520
2.0f  ->  1073741824

It also makes 0.0 come out as all-zero bits, which is quietly convenient everywhere — a zeroed block of memory is a block of zero floats. (The trick only works for positives; the sign bit is a separate flag, not a two's-complement sign, so negative floats sort backwards. That's a real wart and nobody pretends otherwise.)

Read one yourselfThe format is now fully specified

You have everything: sign, exponent minus 127, and a fraction that means 1. followed by those bits. So decode this by hand — no program.

Do this with a pen 
0 10000010 01000000000000000000000

Sign, exponent, fraction. What number is it?

Walk it

Sign 0 → positive.

Exponent field 10000010 = 130. Minus the bias 127 = 3.

Fraction 0100… → the value is 1.01 in binary. The first place after the point is worth ½, the second ¼. So 1.01 = 1 + 0 + ¼ = 1.25.

1.25 × 23 = 1.25 × 8 = 10.0

Confirm it: those bits are 0x41200000, and memcpy them into a float and print — you get 10.

A shortcut worth knowing now

printf can print a float in exactly this form, and it saves you the bit-fiddling. %a prints hexadecimal floating point:

printf("%a\n", 0.5);   ->  0x1p-1
printf("%a\n", 3.0);   ->  0x1.8p+1
printf("%a\n", 0.1);   ->  0x1.999999999999ap-4

Read it as 1.8 in hex, times 2 to the +1. It shows you the significand and the exponent directly, with no rounding and no decimal lies. When a float confuses you, %a is the honest answer.

The consequenceNow everything follows from one picture

Here is the part that matters. Everything else in this lesson — every bug, every rule, every piece of advice you've ever been given about floats — is a consequence of what I'm about to draw. So don't skim it.

Between 1.0 and 2.0, the exponent is fixed at 0 and only the 23 fraction bits vary. So there are exactly 223 = 8,388,608 floats in that interval, evenly spread.

Predict, and be precise

Now go up one interval, from 2.0 to 4.0. The exponent is 1 and the same 23 fraction bits vary.

How many floats are in that interval, and how far apart are they?

Then answer the same for 1000 to 2000, and for ten million to twenty million.

The answer, and it is the crux of this rung

The same 8,388,608 values — spread over twice the distance. So the gap between neighbouring floats doubles. And it doubles again at 4, and again at 8, forever.

Measured on your machine, with nextafterf, which hands you the very next representable float:

gap just above 1.0        0.000000119
gap just above 1000.0     0.000061035
gap just above 10000000   1.0

Up at ten million, consecutive floats are one apart. There are no floats between 10000000 and 10000001. None. The number line, as far as a float is concerned, has holes in it a full unit wide.

Say it as one sentence and carry it out of this rung:

A float's precision is relative, not absolute. It holds about seven significant digits everywhere — which means it holds no fixed number of decimal places anywhere.

That is the whole rung. Everything from here is you noticing where it bites.

Use it immediately

Given that the gap up at ten million is 1.0, predict both of these:

float x = 10000000.0f;
printf("%s\n", (x + 0.4f == x) ? "unchanged" : "changed");

float y = 16777216.0f;      /* this is 2 to the 24 */
printf("%.1f\n", y + 1.0f);
Both, with the reason 
unchanged
16777216.0

Adding 0.4 to ten million produces a true answer of 10000000.4, and there is no float there. The nearest float is 10000000. So the result of the addition is the number you started with — the addition happened and changed nothing.

The second is the same thing at the exact boundary. Below 224 the gap is 1, so every whole number is representable. At 224 = 16777216 the gap becomes 2, and the odd numbers vanish. A float can count integers exactly up to sixteen million and then it starts skipping.

Which is a rule you can now derive instead of memorise: a float holds integers exactly up to 224; a double up to 253 — about 9 quadrillion. It's just the significand width, appearing in a costume.

Back to the crime sceneWhy 0.1 was doomed before any addition happened

Now we can answer the opening properly, and the answer has nothing to do with addition. 0.1 was already wrong on its own.

Start somewhere you're sure of: write ⅓ in decimal. 0.3333… forever. It never terminates, so if I give you eight boxes to write digits in, you must stop and hand back something that isn't ⅓.

Why does that happen — and to which fractions?

⅓ doesn't terminate in decimal but ¼ does (0.25), and ⅕ does (0.2). What decides it?

Then, having answered that: which fractions terminate in binary? And is 1/10 one of them?

The rule, and the verdict on 0.1

A fraction terminates in a base exactly when its denominator is built only from that base's prime factors. Ten is 2 × 5, so in decimal, denominators made of 2s and 5s terminate: ½, ¼, ⅕, 1/20 all fine. Anything with a 3 in the denominator repeats forever.

Two has only one prime factor: 2. So in binary, only denominators that are powers of two terminate. ½, ¼, ⅛, 3/16 — exact. And one tenth is 1/(2×5), and that 5 is fatal.

0.1 is a repeating fraction in binary. It is not merely hard to store — it does not terminate, so no finite number of bits can hold it, in the same way and for the same reason that no finite number of decimal digits holds one third.

Do it by hand, because you should see it repeat with your own eyes. To write a fraction in binary you repeatedly double it: if you pass 1, the digit is 1 and you drop the 1; otherwise the digit is 0.

0.1 × 2 = 0.2   ->  0
0.2 × 2 = 0.4   ->  0
0.4 × 2 = 0.8   ->  0
0.8 × 2 = 1.6   ->  1   (keep 0.6)
0.6 × 2 = 1.2   ->  1   (keep 0.2)
0.2 × 2 = 0.4   ->  0        <-- we have been here before
0.4 × 2 = 0.8   ->  0
0.8 × 2 = 1.6   ->  1
...

0.1  =  0.0 0011 0011 0011 0011 ...   forever

And now dump the real thing. Here is 0.1f out of your machine:

0.1f    0 01111011 10011001100110011001101
                   ^^^^ ^^^^ ^^^^ ^^^^ ^^^^

Exponent 123 − 127 = −4, so the value is 1.1001100110011… × 2−4, which slides the point four places left and gives you 0.00011001100110011… — the repeating 0011 you just derived, character for character. The bits are the arithmetic you did on paper.

Look at the last four though: 1101, where the pattern says 1100. The machine ran out of room and rounded up rather than chopping. So 0.1f is not 0.1, and it is not slightly less than 0.1 either — it is slightly more:

printf("%.30f\n", (double)0.1f);
0.100000001490116119384765625000
One question about that number, and it's the one people get wrong

That decimal expansion stops. It doesn't trail off, it doesn't repeat — it ends, exactly, at …625. Why is it exact?

Because floats are not fuzzy — and this is the misconception to kill

Every float is some whole number divided by a power of two. Powers of two have only 2s in the denominator, and 10 = 2 × 5 contains a 2 — so every float terminates in decimal, exactly. Always.

Which means 0.1f is not "approximately 0.1", in the way a measurement is approximate. It is precisely and exactly 0.100000001490116119384765625, a specific rational number, the same one on every machine, with no uncertainty anywhere.

So burn this sentence in: floats are not imprecise. They are exact numbers — just not the ones you asked for. Nothing about them is random or fuzzy or hardware-dependent. Every operation is defined to compute the true answer and then round it to the nearest representable float, deterministically. The error is not noise. It is a lookup: you asked for a point, and you got the nearest hole in the sieve.

So the opening mystery dissolves. 0.1 becomes the nearest double above it. 0.2 becomes the nearest double to it. Their exact sum is a number that isn't representable either, so it rounds again — and it lands one step away from the nearest double to 0.3. Three roundings, three different sieves, and the last one misses by a single step at the bottom of the mantissa:

0.1 + 0.2  =  0.30000000000000004441
0.3        =  0.29999999999999998890

Nothing went wrong. Everything did exactly what it promised.

What falls outFour rules you can now derive, not memorise

Each of these is the crux wearing a different hat. Try to see which hat before you read the explanation.

1. Never compare floats with ==. You already know why: the value you typed and the value stored differ, so equality asks a question about the sieve rather than about the number. Instead ask are these close enough — but do it carefully:

Spot the bug in the standard advice

The usual fix you'll be given is: if (fabs(a - b) < 1e-9). Given what you now know about gaps, when does that fail?

It fails wherever the gap is bigger than your epsilon 
at 0.3:   |(0.1+0.2) - 0.3|  = 5.55e-17    < 1e-9  ->  passes
at 1e9:   |(1e9+0.1+0.2) - (1e9+0.3)| = 1.19e-07  < 1e-9  ->  FAILS

Same computation, shifted up the number line. A fixed absolute epsilon is a fixed-point idea, and you already killed fixed point at the top of this lesson. Up at a billion, consecutive doubles are further apart than 1e-9, so two "equal" numbers can never be within it — and far below 1, an epsilon of 1e-9 is so coarse it calls genuinely different numbers equal.

Precision is relative, so the tolerance must be relative too:

if (fabs(a - b) <= 1e-9 * fmax(fabs(a), fabs(b)))   /* passes at both scales */

And when a and b can be near zero, you need an absolute floor as well, because relative tolerance collapses there. There's no one-liner that's right everywhere — which is itself the lesson. The comparison depends on the magnitudes you actually have, and only you know those.

2. Never accumulate. Every addition rounds. Do it ten times and the roundings compound:

float s = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < 10; i++) s += 0.1f;
printf("%.10f  %s\n", s, (s == 1.0f) ? "== 1" : "!= 1");

1.0000001192  != 1

So for (float x = 0; x != 1.0f; x += 0.1f) is an infinite loop. It steps straight over 1.0 and keeps going forever. Loop with an integer counter and multiply — x = i * 0.1f — two roundings instead of ten.

3. Never store money in a float. Cents are hundredths; a hundred is 2 × 2 × 5 × 5; the 5s mean nearly every price you can write is unrepresentable. Add up a million transactions and the drift is real money that a real auditor will ask you about. Store money as an integer count of cents, or a decimal type. This is not superstition — it is the terminating-fraction rule from ten minutes ago.

4. Subtracting near-equal numbers destroys your accuracy. This one is worth its own moment, because it's the one that ruins scientific code silently.

Predict 
float big = 1.0e8f;
printf("%g\n", (big + 1.0f) - big);

Algebra says the answer is 1. What does the machine say, and why?

Zero — and here's the shape of the damage

Up at 108 the gap between floats is about 8, so big + 1 can't move — it rounds straight back to big. Subtract big and you get 0. The information about the 1 was destroyed at the moment of the addition, and the subtraction just revealed the crater.

The general form is nastier because it looks fine. Take two doubles that agree to twelve digits and subtract them:

x = 1234567.891234
y = 1234567.891233
x - y  =  1.0000076e-06        (the true answer is 1.0e-06)

Each input carried a relative error around 10−16 — beautiful. But the leading digits cancelled, and the answer is a thousand times smaller than the inputs, so the same absolute error is now enormous relative to the result. You've lost six digits of accuracy in a single subtraction. This is called catastrophic cancellation, and the defence is to rearrange the algebra so the near-equal subtraction never happens.

The last two bit patternsWhat to do with the reserved exponents

One loose end, and you can predict the design. The exponent field is 8 bits, so it runs 0 to 255 — but two of those patterns, all-zeros and all-ones, were held back and given special meaning.

You're the designer again

Your format has to answer 1.0/0.0, and it has to answer 0.0/0.0, and it must never trap or crash — remember rung 0, there is nothing there at runtime to catch anything.

What do you do with those two spare exponent patterns?

Infinity, and the value that isn't a number

All-ones exponent with a zero fraction means infinity, signed. All-ones with a nonzero fraction means NaN — Not a Number — the result of a question with no answer: 0/0, ∞ − ∞, the square root of a negative.

double inf = 1.0 / 0.0;      ->  inf
double bad = inf - inf;      ->  nan

Both are ordinary values. They flow through arithmetic, they get returned from functions, they propagate: anything touching a NaN becomes a NaN, so one bad input at the start quietly poisons a whole computation to the end. Nothing stops. Nothing warns.

And NaN gets one deliberately strange property:

nan == nan   ->   false

It is the only value in C that is not equal to itself. Which sounds like sabotage until you see what it buys — it makes the NaN test free, in a language that has no exceptions to throw:

if (x != x) { /* x is NaN */ }

(All-zeros exponent is the other reserved pattern: it gives you exact zero, and the very tiny numbers just below the normal range.)

float or double — the practical answer

float gives you 24 significand bits, about 7 decimal digits. double gives you 53, about 16. Your machine's own report: FLT_DIG 6, DBL_DIG 15 — the digits it will guarantee to round-trip.

Use double unless you have a reason not to. C already agrees with you: an unsuffixed constant like 3.14 is a double, and double is what the maths library speaks. Reach for float when you have millions of values and memory bandwidth is the bottleneck — graphics, audio buffers, neural network weights — which is a decision about cache, not about numbers. That's rung 22.

Your turnSix holes to find

Write your prediction before running each one. The prediction is the exercise; the program is only the marking.

1. Decode these four bytes by hand — sign, exponent minus 127, fraction — then check with memcpy into a float. Remember rung 1: they're in memory order.

00 00 c0 3f

2. Use bits() on 0.1f, 0.2f and 0.4f. The fraction fields should be nearly identical. Explain in one sentence why they are, and what differs.

3. Find, with a loop and no arithmetic cleverness, the smallest float value of x for which x + 1.0f == x. Start at 1 and double. Then explain the number you get without using the word "float".

4. This one has been shipped to production more than once. What does this print, and how would you write it so it terminates?

for (float x = 0.0f; x != 1.0f; x += 0.1f)
    printf("%f\n", x);

Don't run it without a way to stop it — Ctrl-C.

5. A shop's till adds up 100 items priced at 0.10 each, as a double, and compares the total to 10.00 with ==. Predict whether it matches. Then predict what happens for 0.25 each, and say why the two cases differ before you run them.

6. The architect's question. You're storing GPS coordinates — latitude and longitude in degrees — and you need about one metre of resolution. One degree of latitude is roughly 111 km. A colleague writes float lat;. Is that enough? Compute the gap between floats near 45.0 and turn it into metres before you answer. Then say what you'd use.

Check yourself — after all six are written down

1. In memory order 00 00 c0 3f is little-endian, so the number is 0x3fc00000: sign 0, exponent field 01111111 = 127 → 20, fraction 100… → 1.1 binary = 1.5. Answer: 1.5.

2. Identical fraction fields, exponents one apart:

0.1f   0 01111011 10011001100110011001101
0.2f   0 01111100 10011001100110011001101
0.4f   0 01111101 10011001100110011001101

Doubling a number in binary changes only where the point sits, not the digits — exactly as doubling in decimal would if the base were 2. So the significand is untouched and only the exponent moves. Which also tells you 0.2f == 2 * 0.1f is true: multiplying by two is exact, it just increments the exponent.

3. 16777216. Without the word float: it's 224, the point where you have used up all 24 bits of your significand on the whole part, so the next value you can name is two away, and one is no longer a distance the format can express.

4. It never stops. 0.1f is slightly above one tenth, so the drift is upward and the tenth step overshoots — watch the last two values printed:

0.0000000 0.1000000 ... 0.7000000 0.8000001 0.9000001 1.0000001

x steps from 0.9000001 straight to 1.0000001 and never touches 1.0, so x != 1.0f stays true and the loop runs off toward infinity printing forever. Fix it by counting in integers:

for (int i = 0; i < 10; i++) {
    float x = i * 0.1f;
    printf("%f\n", x);
}

Note the second virtue of this version, beyond terminating: each x is two roundings away from the truth instead of i roundings deep.

5. The 0.10 case fails; the 0.25 case matches exactly. 0.25 is ¼ — a power of two in the denominator, so it is stored exactly, and sums of it are exact until you exceed 253. 0.10 has a 5 in its denominator, so it is stored as a near neighbour and a hundred additions accumulate a hundred roundings. Both results are perfectly predictable; neither is a bug in the machine.

6. Not enough, and it's the kind of thing that ships. Near 45.0 the exponent is 5 (32 ≤ 45 < 64), so the gap is 25 × 2−23 = 2−18 ≈ 0.0000038 degrees. Times 111 km per degree is about 0.42 metres. So a single stored coordinate is borderline — it happens to squeak under a metre — but you have no headroom at all, and the moment you do arithmetic on it (differences between points, a running sum, a projection) the roundings compound past a metre immediately. And the same float at longitude 120 has twice the gap. Use double: near 45 its gap is about 7 × 10−15 degrees, under a nanometre, and the question never comes up again. The general habit: don't ask "is float precise enough for this number?" — compute the gap at the magnitudes you'll actually hold, and compare it to the resolution you need.

Before you move on. This lesson will have felt clear, which is exactly the danger — reading a derivation feels like owning it, and it isn't the same thing. So test it cold, tomorrow, with nothing open: explain why 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3 without saying "rounding error". If your explanation doesn't pass through which fractions terminate in base two and a fixed number of bits means finitely many values, you have the slogan and not the model. And keep the one sentence: precision is relative, not absolute. Every rule in the second half of this lesson is that sentence in disguise, and you should be able to unmask any of them.
